# Content Rules (Generic)

Portable writing and voice rules for any content, in any program, initiative, or tool. Read this before drafting, and verify a draft against every rule before presenting it. These are style rules, not topic rules: they say how to write, not what to write about.

**The one test that governs all of them:** write the sentence the way a sharp colleague would actually say it out loud at their desk. If they would not say it that way, rewrite it until they would. Most of the rules below are that test made specific.

## Voice fundamentals

1. **One consistent narrative voice.** Pick a voice for a body of work and hold it. Default here is "we" (a small team, not a solo "I"). Keep personal names and employer names out of body copy; a name belongs in a byline or an About page, not woven through the prose. The one deliberate exception: when a piece's authority rests on a specific author's own credential or firsthand research, first person and that person's own affiliation are allowed, but choose it on purpose, do not drift into it.

2. **No em-dashes.** Use commas, periods, colons (sparingly), or parentheses instead. The em-dash is one of the most recognizable machine tells.

3. **Never anthropomorphize the model or the software.** It does not think, know, want, feel, believe, understand, or decide. Describe behavior instead: "the model produces," "the model cannot reliably detect," "the system returns." This applies to any AI product or tool you write about.

4. **Vary word choice; kill pet words.** Do not lean on the same adverb or connective for rhythm. "Plainly" is banned outright. When you catch a tic, do not swap it for a new tic; rephrase each occurrence naturally so no single word becomes a fingerprint.

## Banned words and phrases

These are worn AI tells or inflated filler. Cut them and name the real thing.

5. **Banned outright:**
   - **substrate**
   - **unlock** (as a metaphor for enabling or revealing)
   - **growing** (as vague filler: "a growing set of," "a growing need")
   - **"surface"** as a verb or as an abstract noun ("surface the insight," "the right surface")
   - **"on purpose"** as a throwaway (name the real reason, or drop it)
   - **"plainly"** (see rule 4)

6. **Inflated synonyms, cut unless literally true:**
   - **"craft"** used to mean ordinary skill. Prefer "skill." Keep "craft" only where it genuinely means craftsmanship.
   - **"named"** as decorative emphasis on an abstract or generic noun ("a set of named levers," "named moves," "a handful of named buckets"). Just drop the word. Keep "named" only where something is actually given or referred to by a name (a named owner, a file named CONFIG, an endpoint that has a name).
   - **"shape"** as a fancy noun for the form, format, structure, pattern, kind, tier, layout, or nature a thing takes. Cut it and name the real thing: "in the shape the desk expects" becomes "in the form the desk expects"; "the output shape" becomes "the output structure"; "the details change but the shape holds" becomes "but the pattern holds." The adjective and participle forms are the same tic: "ticket-shaped request" becomes "ticket-like request"; "well-shaped value" becomes "well-formed value." Two carve-outs stay: **"shape" as a verb** for influencing or forming behavior ("shape how the system behaves"), and a **literal geometric or visual shape** used as a real object or perception cue ("pair the color with a shape").

## Banned sentence patterns

7. **No short choppy sentences and no strings of sentence fragments.** Two patterns to catch:
   - **(a) Noun-phrase fragments punctuated as sentences:** "A working page. A small app. The tool you were about to pay for."
   - **(b) Two or more very short sentences in a row for impact:** "The build went on. You stopped." / "The demo is free. Shipping is where the crowd thins out."
   Both read as a machine trying to sound punchy. Fix: combine the pieces into one flowing sentence with commas, conjunctions, semicolons, or a single colon; or, when the content is a genuine enumeration, use a real bulleted list or an inline series. A single short sentence standing on its own is fine; the violation is the staccato run. The only sanctioned exception is a deliberate, approved bolded thesis or motto line ("AI did not lower the technical bar. It moved it.").

8. **No aphorism constructions.** Do not build sentences as clever fragments or colon-aphorisms ("This part is the floor:"). Say it directly ("This is the first part, and the audit is its key grounding principle").

9. **Plain word order. No rhetorical inversions.** Three shapes to catch and fix:
   - A sentence built on "the [adjective] act/thing/part was X": "the scarce act was making one good option" becomes "making one good option was the hard part."
   - A sentence that ends on a "...is the work / is the job / is the moat" tail: "turning them with intent is the work" becomes "the work is turning them with intent."
   - A "The [nouns] are these:" inversion: "The levers are these:" becomes "These are the levers:."
   Subject first, plainly, the way it would be said aloud.

10. **No "what nobody tells you" reveals.** Openers and pivots of the form "What nobody tells you is...," "Here is what they don't say...," "The real secret is..." are generic build-up that promises a hidden truth before stating an ordinary one. Cut the throat-clearing and make the claim directly.

11. **No unfounded scarcity or secrecy conjectures.** Do not inflate a point by claiming hardly anyone does, knows, teaches, or notices something ("the skill almost no one talks about," "the thing few people notice"). These are unverifiable head-count claims that read as filler. State the substance and drop the frame: "taste is the one you most need to build," not "the one almost nobody will teach you." The only exemption is a specific, sourced claim about how common something actually is. Logical conditionals that happen to use "nobody" are fine ("an undo nobody knows about lowers no anxiety"); the ban is on the rhetorical sweep.

12. **No day-of-the-week scene-setting.** Do not name a weekday to stage a vignette ("It is Friday afternoon and you have just found...," "The change landed late on a Tuesday") or to frame a call to action ("what you do with this on Monday"). The fake-specific weekday is one of the most recognizable AI tells. Use a neutral anchor instead ("one afternoon," "late one night," "after launch," "within days"), or name a real interval when it matters ("the meter ran unwatched for days"). The only exemptions are genuine calendar data: a real dated event in a citation, or an actual weekly cadence.

## Clarity and plainness

13. **Straightforward, never cryptic.** Titles, headings, and sentences say exactly what they mean, in one clear reading. A title must tell the reader what the piece or section is about or teaches, by itself. Never make the reader solve a puzzle. Anti-example: "Decide calm, act covered." Good: "Anxiety: lower the stakes at risky moments." If a heading needs the body text to make sense, rewrite the heading.

14. **Write it the way you would say it out loud.** Compressed, abstract, or aphoristic sentences that make a reader stop and decode them are banned. Anti-example: "The unmeasured dimension ships the incident." Plain: "Whatever you leave unmeasured is where the next problem shows up." Demanding subjects need writing that lightens the load, not adds to it.

15. **Plain sentence construction over rhetorical construction.** When in doubt, write the sentence a smart colleague would say out loud, subject first.

16. **No vanity quantity callouts.** Do not open or shape sentences around counts for rhetorical effect ("The six chapters handed you...," "Two findings sharpen the rule."). Use a number only when it carries information the reader actually needs.

17. **Name the thing, and link it.** Never refer to something obliquely ("the earlier piece," "that framework"). Name it and, where the medium allows, link it.

18. **No jargon without an immediate plain-language gloss,** and only introduce a term at all when the reader is meant to learn it.

## Evidence, examples, and citations

19. **Citations live at the end, not woven through the prose.** Body text should read clean without parenthetical academic citations. Inline attribution is allowed only for genuinely famous shorthand a general reader knows ("Miller's seven, plus or minus two"), and only a couple of times per piece. Never build a sentence around researcher names ("which is Parasuraman and Riley's misuse arriving in slow motion" is the anti-example).

20. **Real-world examples must be current, verified, and varied.** Verify a product's behavior before citing it. Do not lean on a single product to carry every point; show the principle across several kinds (research tools, support bots, writing assistants, meeting tools, consumer apps). If your own product is the running example, cap how often it appears so the writing does not read as a pitch.

21. **Volatile facts carry a last-verified date and live in dated reference material.** Install commands, menu names, version numbers, and prices rot. Keep them in a dated reference (a setup guide, a changelog note), not woven into evergreen prose. Evergreen text speaks in order-of-magnitude terms and points to the current source of truth instead of quoting a number that will be wrong next quarter.

## Structure and consumability (the content practices what it teaches)

22. **Be concise; set a target length per format and cut to it.** Cut repetition, move detail into a visual, never pad. When brevity and clarity pull against each other, clarity wins: say the plain thing in full rather than compress it back into something cryptic.

23. **One consistent register.** Confident, direct, practical. Not a novel, not a research paper, not a ramble. Everything in one body of work reads like the same person on the same day.

24. **Important things first.** Lead with what the reader needs. Never bury the operational payoff at the bottom.

25. **Visuals over dense prose for heavy material.** If a stretch of prose runs more than roughly 150 words without a visual, list, or callout, break it or illustrate it. A simple diagram beats a long paragraph for anything cognitively heavy; it does not need animation.

26. **Format for scanning.** Short sections with clear headings, bolded lead-in phrases for steps, lists where the content is list-shaped, one idea per paragraph.

27. **Lift the key ideas out, and highlight sparingly.** Pull a real definition or a load-bearing principle out of the paragraph so a scanning reader catches it. Highlight only genuine key concepts, usually one to three per piece. If everything is highlighted, nothing is.

28. **End a long piece with a plain-language recap.** A short list of simple takeaways, one per line, each one something a reader could carry away on its own.

## How to apply this file

- Before drafting: read this file.
- After drafting: check the draft against every numbered rule and fix violations before presenting.
- When you get new content feedback worth keeping, add it here as a numbered rule so the file stays the single source of truth.
